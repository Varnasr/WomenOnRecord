    Where a woman's case is lost

    Violence against a woman has to pass several steps before it can end in a verdict, and at each step some cases go no further. This page follows the steps in order. Each step gives the figure from the source that measures it and links to the page that holds the detail.

    The survey figures, state by state

    2. Telling someone

    Researchers who studied the same survey found 141 women who had gone to the police, among 17,765 who had faced physical or sexual violence from a husband or partner (Ghoshal and others, BMC Global and Public Health, 17 April 2024, Table 3). That is about 8 in every 1,000, counted before the survey's weights are applied.

    3. Going to the police

    The law requires the police to record information about a cognizable offence, at any police station, whatever the place where the offence was committed (BNSS s.173(1)). The Supreme Court held in Lalita Kumari v Government of Uttar Pradesh ((2014) 2 SCC 1, decided 12 November 2013) that "Registration of FIR is mandatory under Section 154 of the Code, if the information discloses commission of a cognizable offence". The BNSS now allows a preliminary enquiry of up to fourteen days before registration, with the prior permission of an officer of the rank of Deputy Superintendent or above, for offences punishable with three years or more and less than seven (s.173(3)).

    A public servant who fails to record information about certain sexual offences can be imprisoned for six months to two years (BNS s.199(c)). The list leaves out sexual harassment (s.75), stalking (s.78) and sexual intercourse by deceitful means (s.69).

    A study by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative and the Association for Advocacy and Legal Initiatives followed 14 rape cases in Uttar Pradesh. The police registered an FIR for rape in 11 of them, after 2 to 228 days, and in every one of the 11 only after the complaint was taken to senior officers or a court ordered it (Barriers in Accessing Justice, 2020, page 1).

    4. Being counted

    A case the police record is counted by NCRB once, under the offence that carries the longest sentence, and the other offences in the same FIR do not appear in the count. NCRB's 2016 edition said this rule did not apply to its chapter on crimes against women. The same edition's figures show the rule at work in that chapter for the Dowry Prohibition Act, where about 38,632 FIRs carried the charge and 9,683 were counted. No edition since 2016 repeats the exception.

    Where the law itself leaves gaps

    The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita keeps the marital exception. Section 63, Exception 2, reads, "Sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape." A woman of eighteen or more who is raped by her husband cannot bring a case of rape, and no such case can be counted as rape.

    Section 63 opens with the words "A man is said to commit 'rape'", and the offence it defines is committed against a woman. Rape of anyone else cannot be recorded as rape under it.

    The law

    Cases and reports that changed the law

    Each is given by its legal title and what changed. No survivor is named or described, as the Supreme Court directed in Nipun Saxena, below.

    • Tuka Ram v State of Maharashtra ((1979) 2 SCC 143, AIR 1979 SC 185, 15 September 1978). The Supreme Court set aside the High Court's conviction of two policemen for rape in a police station and acquitted them. The Law Commission, asked by the government to study the law on rape, cited the judgment in its 84th Report (1980) among "recent judicial decisions" that had "provoked a debate as to the deficiencies" of the law (para 1.4). The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983 (Act 43 of 1983, in force from 25 December 1983) made it an offence to publish the identity of a rape victim (IPC s.228A) and required rape trials to be held in camera (CrPC s.327(2)), as the Supreme Court records in Nipun Saxena (paras 6 to 8). The same Act added a presumption that the woman did not consent in certain prosecutions for rape (Evidence Act s.114A), according to the Law Commission's 185th Report (2003, page 265).
    • Vishaka v State of Rajasthan ((1997) 6 SCC 241, 13 August 1997). The Supreme Court laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at work, to be treated as law until Parliament legislated. Parliament did so in the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (Act 14 of 2013), in force from 9 December 2013.
    • The Report of the Committee on Amendments to Criminal Law, chaired by Justice J.S. Verma, 23 January 2013. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 (Act 13 of 2013, in force from 3 February 2013) followed. It made a public servant's failure to record an FIR for listed offences punishable, and added acid attack, sexual harassment, voyeurism and stalking as offences.
    • Lalita Kumari v Government of Uttar Pradesh ((2014) 2 SCC 1, 12 November 2013). A Constitution Bench held that the police must register an FIR when the information discloses a cognizable offence.
    • Independent Thought v Union of India (W.P.(C) 382 of 2013, AIR 2017 SC 4904, 11 October 2017). The Supreme Court read the marital exception up to a wife of eighteen, from a wife of fifteen. The BNS wrote eighteen into the text.
    • Nipun Saxena v Union of India ((2019) 2 SCC 703, 11 December 2018). The Supreme Court directed that no one may publish the name of a rape victim, or any fact that could lead to her being identified.

    Who else works on these gaps

    These organisations publish work on the steps above. Each line says what it covers, from its own pages. The list is not complete, and inclusion is no endorsement.

    • India Justice Report ranks the states on the capacity of their police, courts, prisons and legal aid. Its 2025 report found women were 12.3% of the police in January 2023 (page 53).
    • Status of Policing in India Report is a series of surveys of police and the public by Common Cause and Lokniti-CSDS, from 2018 to 2026.
    • Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative works on police accountability and published the study of 14 rape cases cited above.
    • Development Data Lab publishes records of 81 million district court cases from 2010 to 2018, free to download, so a case can be followed from filing to judgment.
    • DAKSH researches the courts, including how long cases wait.
    • Human Rights Advocacy and Research Foundation published How She Figures in August 2025, a reading of NCRB's figures and definitions for crimes against women from 1953 to 2022, in English and Tamil.
    • CEDA, Ashoka University published an analysis by Sneha Thomas of what NCRB's 2024 figures miss, including the counting rule, on 22 June 2026.
    • Observer Research Foundation published Ramanath Jha's essay on the limits of NCRB's counting, including the counting rule, on 30 June 2026.

    The Ministry of Women and Child Development reported on 29 March 2025 that 14,658 Women Help Desks had been set up in police stations, 13,743 of them headed by women officers, and described an online tool, the Investigation Tracking System for Sexual Offences, that monitors whether police investigations of sexual assault are completed on time (India's Commitment to Women's Safety, page 4). No figures from that tool are published.

    The only count this site has found of what the rule hides is NCRB's own, for the Dowry Prohibition Act in 2016, set out on the counting page. A count for any other crime would need the offences listed in each FIR, which are not public.
