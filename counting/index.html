    Data and method

    How the numbers are made

    Every crime figure on this site is a count of cases that police recorded and NCRB compiled. This page sets out the rules behind those counts, in NCRB's own words, and what the rules leave out.

    One case, one heading

    NCRB counts each First Information Report (FIR) once, however many offences it lists. Its notes to Crime in India 2024 say that if many offences are registered in a single FIR, "only the most heinous crime i.e. the one that attracts maximum punishment will be considered as counting unit". NCRB calls this the Principal Offence Rule and says it follows an international standard (Crime in India 2024, Volume 1, General Notes, page vii).

    A case is therefore counted under the offence that carries the longest sentence, and the other offences in the same FIR do not appear in the count. NCRB warns for this reason that "the actual count of each crime head may be different from reported" (page v of the same notes).

    The offences most likely to drop out are the ones with shorter sentences. NCRB's own example, in its 2016 edition, was a charge under the Dowry Prohibition Act filed together with dowry death under IPC section 304B, which "will be counted as Dowry Death only" (Crime in India 2016, General Notes, page xi). If the rule applies to crimes against women, stalking, harassment or cruelty charged in the same FIR as a rape would be counted as rape. NCRB does not publish the other offences listed in each FIR, so nobody outside NCRB can count how many offences are hidden this way.

    NCRB counted it once, for one law. Its 2016 edition says that in the police case database (CCTNS) for 2016, "the FIR count is approximately 38,632" for the Dowry Prohibition Act, "as compared to only 9,683 cases reported in 'Crime in India'", and that the rest "could be tagged with more serious crimes like 304B, 306 and 498A of IPC" (Crime in India 2016, Limitations, page ix). On those figures about one Dowry Prohibition Act charge in four was counted under that Act. None of the editions from 2017 to 2024 repeats the comparison, for this law or any other.

    The rule gives one more case to think about. A woman murdered by a husband who had also been charged with cruelty (IPC 302 with 498A, now BNS 103 with 85) is counted as murder. Murder is not one of the headings in NCRB's chapter on crimes against women, so if the rule applies to that chapter, such a case is not counted among crimes against women at all. NCRB's notes do not say how it treats these cases. This site shows murders of women on a page of their own, from NCRB's count of murder victims by sex.

    Whether the rule applies to crimes against women

    NCRB's 2016 edition said it did not. Its notes read, "The Principle Offence Rule is not applicable for chapters of Crimes against Women, Crimes Against Children, Crimes Against SC/ST, juveniles in conflict with law, crime against senior citizens etc." The same note gave the example that "Murder with Rape is accounted as Murder" in the general count (Crime in India 2016, General Notes, page xi).

    No later edition repeats the exception. The editions for 2017 to 2021 keep the example that "Murder with Rape is accounted as Murder" and leave the exception out (Crime in India 2017, Limitations, page ix, and the same note in the 2018 to 2021 editions). The editions for 2022 to 2024 leave out the example as well. NCRB has not said whether the exception still holds.

    The 2016 edition's own tables point the other way for one heading. Its chapter on crimes against women prints 9,683 cases under the Dowry Prohibition Act (Table 3A.2), the same figure its Limitations give as the count left after the rule, against about 38,632 FIRs. That matches the rule having been applied in the chapter for that heading in that year, although NCRB does not say so. NCRB publishes nothing that shows how the other headings in the chapter are counted, and this site reports them as NCRB printed them.

    Rape of girls

    In 2016 NCRB's rape figure included rapes of girls charged under sections 4 and 6 of the POCSO Act. From 2017 these cases are counted under POCSO. For that reason this site shows POCSO beside rape wherever rape is shown, and no line on its charts joins the rape figures for 2015 and 2017 across 2016.

    The population behind the rates

    A rate is the number of cases for every lakh women, so it moves when the population estimate moves. NCRB changed the source of its estimate twice in these years. For 2016 and 2017 it used the Registrar General's estimates based on the 2001 Census, from 2018 the Technical Group on Population Projections of November 2019, and from 2020 the Technical Group's report of July 2020. The female population of India in NCRB's tables rose by 3.6% between 2017 and 2018 because of the first change.

    Every estimate from 2018 onward is a projection from the 2011 Census, because no census has been taken since. A rate that rises or falls by a few per cent between years can come from the estimate as much as from the cases.

    What never reaches the police

    The counting rule decides which heading a reported case is filed under. It does not change how many cases are reported. By the survey's measure most violence against women never reaches the police, and no counting rule can show a case that was never reported.

    Counting every offence

    Counting each case once keeps the total equal to the number of cases, and lets rates be compared across years and states. The cost is the detail about everything else in the case. In the United States the FBI's older system followed a similar rule, and its National Incident-Based Reporting System lets police report "up to 10 offenses per incident versus only the most serious offense" (FBI, A Guide to Understanding NIBRS, page 1). The FBI tried to move every police agency to the new system on 1 January 2021, and went on accepting data in either form for 2022 and 2023 because many agencies had not changed over (Congressional Research Service, report R46668, 7 July 2025).

    In India, NCRB's 2016 edition listed as a plan "To generate annual publication data from online CCTNS database from next year" (Crime in India 2016, page xvi). CCTNS is the national network for police case records. NCRB's 2024 notes still describe data entered by State and Union Territory police "at Police Station/District level" into tables that NCRB then compiles (Crime in India 2024, General Notes, page vi).
